EU AI Act Dashboard for Universities and Educators

A practical overview of what the EU AI Act means for higher education policy, teaching, assessment, research support, procurement, and everyday educator use of AI.

This is a course and policy-orientation aid, not legal advice. It distinguishes binding AI Act duties from official guidance and expert commentary. The most time-sensitive update is the 2026 delayed application of high-risk rules for education to 2 December 2027.
Prohibited

Some practices are banned, including emotion inference in education except medical or safety reasons.

High risk

Admissions, learning-outcome evaluation, educational level decisions, exam monitoring.

Transparency

Chatbots, deepfakes, public-interest generated text, synthetic content marking.

Minimal risk

Most ordinary AI use remains allowed, but AI literacy and other laws still matter.

The university task is not to ban AI globally. It is to classify use cases, protect rights, train people, document decisions, and keep human academic judgment visible.

Applies now AI literacy

Providers and deployers must take measures so staff and other relevant persons have sufficient AI literacy. Article 4 has applied since 2 February 2025.

Applies now Prohibitions

Do not use AI for prohibited practices. For education, the clearest red line is biometric emotion inference, unless medical or safety reasons apply.

2 Aug 2026 Transparency

Article 50 duties cover AI interaction notices, synthetic content marking, deepfake disclosure, and public-interest AI-generated text disclosures.

2 Dec 2027 High-risk HE

Current official guidance says stand-alone high-risk systems in education apply from 2 December 2027, with possible earlier trigger if support tools are ready.

What Becomes High Risk in Higher Education

Under Annex III, education and vocational training use cases are high-risk when AI is intended for gatekeeping, learning evaluation, educational-level decisions, or test monitoring.

1

Access and Admission

AI used to determine access, admission, or assignment to educational institutions or programmes.

  • Admissions triage or ranking
  • Scholarship or placement screening
  • Automated programme assignment
2

Learning Outcomes

AI used to evaluate learning outcomes, including where results steer a learner's process.

  • Automated grading or scoring
  • AI feedback that materially guides progression
  • Adaptive systems steering learning paths
3

Level of Education

AI used to assess what level of education a person will receive or be able to access.

  • Placement into tracks or levels
  • Prerequisite or readiness decisions
  • Progression recommendations with real effect
4

Exam Monitoring

AI used to monitor or detect prohibited student behaviour during tests.

  • AI proctoring
  • Cheating detection
  • Behavioural anomaly scoring in exams

University Roles Under the Act

The same university can be a deployer, provider, downstream provider, employer, public body, purchaser, and publisher depending on the AI use case.

D

Deployer

The university uses an AI system under its authority. This is the most common role for bought-in educational technology.

  • Use according to instructions
  • Assign trained human oversight
  • Monitor operation and risks
  • Keep logs where under control
P

Provider or Downstream Provider

The university develops, substantially modifies, brands, or changes the intended purpose of an AI system.

  • Risk management and documentation
  • Conformity assessment where required
  • Lifecycle monitoring and incident handling
  • Contractual access to technical information
E

Educator or Staff User

Educators are often acting on behalf of the deployer. Their practical duties come through institutional policy, training, and tool rules.

  • Use approved tools for student data
  • Check outputs before use
  • Keep human pedagogical judgment
  • Follow assessment disclosure rules

What Educators Need to Know

Educators do not need to become lawyers. They need a reliable classroom rule set for safe, critical, transparent AI use.

L

AI Literacy Is Role-Based

Training should be tailored to the tool and teaching context, not reduced to one generic webinar.

  • What AI tools are approved?
  • What data may be entered?
  • What risks matter in this course?
  • How should students disclose AI use?
A

Assessment Needs Human Judgment

AI can support drafting rubrics, examples, or feedback, but high-stakes evaluation requires human authority and a clear challenge route.

  • No hidden automated grading
  • No AI-only disciplinary conclusions
  • Document how AI supports feedback
  • Keep learning outcomes visible
R

Red Lines for Classrooms

Some AI practices are unsuitable or unlawful in education contexts, especially where students are monitored or profiled.

  • No emotion recognition in education except medical/safety reasons
  • Avoid biometric or behavioural surveillance by default
  • No uploading sensitive data to unapproved tools
  • No non-consensual intimate synthetic content

Policy Building Blocks

A university AI policy should be operational enough for procurement, educators, IT, data protection, research units, and students.

G

Institutional Governance

  • Single AI register with risk classification and owner for each tool.
  • Procurement checklist covering AI Act role, data protection, documentation, logging, human oversight, accessibility, and cybersecurity.
  • Escalation route for suspected high-risk, prohibited, or sensitive AI use.
  • Template for AI literacy records and tool-specific training.
T

Teaching and Assessment Rules

  • Course-level permitted AI use levels and student disclosure rules.
  • Assessment design that tests learning, not only output production.
  • Rules for AI-supported feedback, marking, oral checks, viva, and appeals.
  • Guidance on copyright, citation, hallucination, bias, and accessibility.
R

Research and Innovation

  • Clarify when research/prototyping becomes release, deployment, or service provision.
  • Review fine-tuned models, student-facing research tools, and health/education deployments before launch.
  • Protect personal, confidential, unpublished, and partner data.
  • Document use of general-purpose models and downstream changes.
S

Student Rights and Safeguards

  • Clear notice when high-risk AI assists decisions about students.
  • Human review, challenge, and explanation process for consequential outputs.
  • Non-discriminatory alternatives where surveillance or accessibility concerns arise.
  • Protection against over-reliance, misinformation, bias, and undue profiling.
