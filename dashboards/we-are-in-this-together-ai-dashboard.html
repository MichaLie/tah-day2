We Are in This Together

A classroom philosophy for AI use: academics model transparent practice, students help probe the moving frontier, and the course becomes a disciplined space for finding what works, what fails, and what should become policy.

The role-model move: disclose your own AI use to students, not only to journals. Show prompts, checks, edits, and rejects. Transparency from the front of the room is the strongest signal that AI use is a practice of judgment, not a shortcut.
1
Show the workflowMake AI use visible: purpose, prompt, output, checks, and final human decision.
2
Probe the frontierTest AI on real disciplinary tasks and record where performance is uneven.
3
Share the failuresPut useful outputs and bad outputs side by side, without blame.
4
Update the normTurn observed practice into course guidance, disclosure rules, and assessment design.

The Philosophy in One Sentence

Do not treat AI rules as a document students read once. Treat them as a visible practice that the class learns, tests, and refines together.

Model - test - disclose - revise

Rules work when the practice behind them is visible.

Academics bring disciplinary standards, assessment responsibility, and ethical boundaries. Students bring live tool experience, new workflows, and failure cases. The classroom becomes the safest serious place to discover the difference.

A
Academics model judgmentThey show how expert standards shape prompts, verification, editing, citation, and rejection.
S
Students surface the moving frontierThey often know emerging tools first. That knowledge becomes course evidence, not a hidden advantage.
D

A Simple Disclosure Routine for Academics

Use disclosure as teaching material. Students learn more from seeing a real decision trail than from hearing a general rule.

1

Before class: prepare one trace

Select a real workflow from your teaching or research preparation.

  • Tool or model used, with date.
  • Prompt or task instruction.
  • Output accepted, edited, or rejected.
  • Verification method and final human decision.
2

In class: narrate judgment

Show the messy middle, not only the polished result.

  • Where AI saved time.
  • Where it sounded confident but failed.
  • What domain knowledge was needed.
  • What you would not share with the tool.
3

After class: update the local rule

Turn what the group learned into clearer assignment guidance.

  • Allowed and disallowed uses.
  • Required student disclosure format.
  • Evidence of learning required.
  • Items to retest as tools change.

Example educator disclosure

I used AI to generate three alternative explanations for this concept. I rejected one because it introduced a misleading analogy. I edited the second, checked it against the course reading, and used the third only as a discussion prompt.

Example student disclosure prompt

State where AI entered your process, paste the most important prompt or workflow step, identify one output you changed or rejected, and explain how you verified the final result.

The Culture That Makes Rules Work

Open does not mean careless. Non-judging does not mean uncritical. The culture is serious because failures are visible and discussable.

Signals to build

  • +Successes and failures side by sideUseful outputs, bad outputs, and rejected outputs all become learning evidence.
  • +Student expertise has a legitimate channelStudents can surface tools and workflows without hiding them or pretending they do not matter.
  • +Standards are explicitThe class keeps returning to accuracy, evidence, privacy, fairness, originality, and learning value.
  • +Policy follows observed practiceRules are refreshed when the class learns something real about the domain and the tools.

Patterns to avoid

  • !Hidden educator AI useStudents see a double standard when academics use AI privately but discuss only student restrictions.
  • !Generic permission statementsAllowed or banned is too thin unless students see the why for each task.
  • !Tool novelty as the goalThe point is not to chase every app. The point is to test value and risk in the discipline.
  • !Disclosure without consequencesDisclosure should connect to feedback, assessment evidence, and better rules.
