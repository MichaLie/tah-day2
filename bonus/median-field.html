What you are looking at

Every pixel is a researcher holding a framing — drawn here as a colour on a wheel. Each moment, one of them rethinks: sometimes alone (their colour drifts — new ideas), sometimes with a neighbour (colours blend — normal science), and sometimes by consulting the shared model. The model is trained on the field itself: its answer is, quite literally, the field's average — served back with total confidence.

In mirror mode, consulting it pulls you toward that average. Each researcher gets a genuinely helpful answer — smoother, safer, closer to the consensus — and the field quietly converges until one framing remains. Nobody chose the beige. Everyone just asked the same question of the same machine. (It doesn't take everyone, either: the dose-response curve below puts the cliff at about one consultation in ten.) In challenger mode, the very same machine is asked to argue instead of confirm: each consultation plants a doubt about the average and — above all — hands the question back, provoking a fresh thought of your own. Same tool, same usage rate, opposite fate: at 70% consultation, six seeded replications put the mirror-field at 0.004 ± 0.000 and the challenger-field at 0.988 ± 0.002 — more plural, in fact, than a field that never met the machine at all (0.913 ± 0.061). The distributions do not overlap. And you needn't take those numbers from me: the button below re-derives every one of them in your own browser.

And the thought-bombs: switch them on and click the field. A door card, a sticker by the coffee machine — a small local injection of dissent. Watch how long a bloom of colour survives in each mode. In mirror mode the field swallows it. In challenger mode it takes.

What building it taught me — twice

A confession from the workbench, in two acts. Act one: my first challenger didn't work — or so I measured. I had built it the obvious way, consulting the machine simply pushed you away from the average, and my quick experiments showed the field converging anyway, regrouping on the far side of the wheel at diversity 0.18. I wrote the tidy moral: everyone disagreeing with the same answer, in the same direction, is also a monoculture. The anti-mirror is still a mirror. It felt deep. I liked it.

Act two: a reviewer asked where the statistics were. Building the seeded replication suite she demanded exposed a flaw in my own quick protocol: I had let the machine go stale — retraining it once per 300,000 updates instead of once per 1,400, as the live toy actually does. Against a frozen average, contrarians really do converge on the antipode; my 0.18 was a true number about the wrong experiment. Against a retraining average — one that chases the very crowd fleeing it — the naive anti-mirror churns and stays plural after all: 0.959 ± 0.006 across seeds, reliably below the true challenger's 0.988 ± 0.002, but nowhere near a monoculture. The tidy moral survives as half of itself: the anti-mirror is a mirror only while the mirror stands still.

So the toy's realest lesson is not in the curves. It took exactly one person asking "where are the stats?" to catch the toy-maker doing the very thing the toy warns about — trusting a fluent, satisfying story from a single unreplicated run. The failed challenger stays in the code as an ablation, the failed protocol stays in this paragraph, and the section you are reading was corrected by peer review. That is the mechanism working.

Methods, transparently

Everything below is implemented in this page's source — view it, audit it, break it. The field is a 64 × 64 torus (4,096 agents). Agent i holds a framing θi ∈ [0, 2π). Each update picks one agent uniformly at random; with AI-share s, it then does one of:

MoveProbabilityUpdate rule
Own thinking0.3 · (1 − s)θ ← θ + ε, ε ≈ 𝒩(0, 0.35) (sum of 4 uniforms)
Peer exchange0.7 · (1 − s)θ ← θ + 0.35 · d(θj, θ), random Moore neighbour j
Machine · mirrors (one of, by mode)θ ← θ + 0.50 · d(θM, θ)
Machine · challengerθ ← θ − 0.05 · d(θM, θ) + 𝒩(0, 1.1)
Machine · anti-mirror (ablation)θ ← θ − 0.30 · d(θM, θ) + 𝒩(0, 0.5)

d(a, b) is the wrapped signed angular difference. The model's answer θM is the direction of an exponential moving average (rate 0.05, applied once per 1,400 updates) of the field's mean resultant vector — the machine is always slightly behind the field it was trained on. This retraining rhythm is identical in the live toy and in every experiment below; a detail that matters more than it looks, because an earlier console protocol retrained the model 200× less often, and the confession above is what that cost. Diversity is the circular variance V = 1 − R, where R is the length of the field's mean resultant vector: V = 1 when framings cover the wheel, V = 0 when one framing remains. Randomness comes from a seeded mulberry32 PRNG, so every experiment below is exactly reproducible from its seed. Thought-bombs are disabled during experiments.

What it is for — and what it is not

This is an intuition pump, not evidence about the world. The real findings live in the guide: a controlled study in which generative AI raised individual creativity while reducing the collective diversity of what the group produced (Doshi & Hauser 2024), and the argument that AI monocultures create "illusions of understanding" — producing more while exploring less (Messeri & Crockett 2024). The toy exists so you can feel the mechanism those papers measure — and, since a sharp reviewer asked, so you can verify that the feeling comes from the stated mechanism and not from theatrical noise.

Honesty note: a 4,096-agent circle-of-hues is not a model of science. It has no truth, no data, no incentives — only imitation and its cure. The constants in the methods table were chosen by hand to make the mechanism visible, not fitted to anything; the claim worth exporting is the ordering of the three curves, not any particular number. Treat every slider here the way the guide teaches you to treat every model: useful exactly to the extent that you remember what it leaves out.
