# Student C

Lecture capture technology has changed higher education over the past decade. This essay weighs its benefits and costs and concludes that its effect is positive when it is used to complement, rather than replace, active teaching.

On the positive side, recorded lectures support revision and accessibility. Students who are non-native speakers can pause and re-watch difficult passages, and students with disabilities benefit from being able to control pacing. Morris et al. (2019) found that students used recordings mainly for revision around assessment periods rather than as a substitute for attendance, which complicates the common assumption that recording empties the lecture hall.

On the negative side, there is evidence that some students reduce attendance, and lecturers report that recording can make live sessions feel flat. It is also true that a recording of a passive lecture simply preserves a passive lecture; the technology does not improve poor teaching.

The evidence on attendance is genuinely mixed: while some studies report a decline, Morris et al. found no consistent relationship, so it would be an overstatement to say recording causes disengagement. The more defensible claim is that the effect depends on how teaching is designed around it.

Overall, lecture capture is positive because its clearest, best-evidenced benefits — revision and accessibility — accrue to exactly the students who need them most, while its costs can be managed through course design.

Word count: ~230. References: Morris, N. P., Swinnerton, B. and Coop, T. (2019). Lecture recordings to support learning. *British Journal of Educational Technology.*
