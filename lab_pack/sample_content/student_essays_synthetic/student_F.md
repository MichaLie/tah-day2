# Student F

This essay examines the impact of open educational resources (OER) — freely licensed textbooks and materials — on higher education over the last decade, and argues that their effect has been positive but uneven.

The strongest case for OER is cost and equity. Commercial textbooks are expensive, and there is evidence that some students do not buy required texts because of price. Colvard, Watson and Park (2018) found that adopting OER improved course grades and reduced failure rates, with the largest gains for Pell-eligible (lower-income) students. If this finding generalizes, OER could narrow attainment gaps, not merely save money.

There are, however, real limitations. Quality varies, because anyone can publish an open resource; academic staff must spend time evaluating and curating materials, which is often unrecognized labour. Sustainability is also a concern: an open textbook that no one updates slowly goes out of date.

A fair assessment must weigh a well-evidenced equity benefit against a diffuse quality-assurance cost. Because the benefit falls on the students least well served by the status quo, and the cost can be addressed institutionally through review processes, I conclude the effect is positive — provided universities invest in curation rather than assuming "free" means "finished".

Word count: ~215. References: Colvard, N. B., Watson, C. E. and Park, H. (2018). The impact of open educational resources on various student success metrics. *International Journal of Teaching and Learning in Higher Education.*
