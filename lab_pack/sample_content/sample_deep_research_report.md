## Question: How should universities respond to student use of generative AI in assessment?

### 1. Student use is now near-universal
Surveys show that student adoption of generative AI has become the norm rather than the exception. The HEPI Student Generative AI Survey 2026 reports that 95% of students use AI in at least one way and 94% use it for assessed work [1]. Adoption has accelerated sharply: the share of students putting AI-generated text directly into assessed work rose from 3% in 2024 to over 40% in 2026 [2].

### 2. Detection is not a reliable solution
AI-text detectors remain unreliable as sole evidence in misconduct cases. A 2026 study by Van Vlasselaer et al. in the *International Journal for Educational Integrity* concluded that no detector should be used as the sole basis for an academic-integrity decision [3]. At the same time, a large controlled evaluation found that the detector Pangram achieved a false-positive rate below 0.5%, the only tool tested to meet that threshold [4]. Detector performance is also unstable across model releases: when a new frontier model launched in early 2026, one leading detector's recall dropped to around one-third until it was retrained [5].

### 3. Detection tools eliminate the need for assessment change
Because the best detectors are now highly accurate, institutions that deploy them can continue to rely on traditional unsupervised essays without significant risk [6].

### 4. Courts are beginning to intervene
In February 2026, a New York court overturned a university's finding of AI-assisted plagiarism that had been based on a detector score, ordering the student's record expunged [7].

### 5. Recommendation
Universities should combine assessment redesign, clear disclosure requirements, and process evidence, treating any detector output as a prompt for conversation rather than proof.
