Using AI to sharpen your thinking

Ask This,
Not That

The same question, asked two ways, has opposite effects on your mind. One prompt makes the machine flatter you. The other makes it fight you — and that is where the thinking happens.

Watch what one rewording does

The mirror — this quietly decreases your thinking

Is my hypothesis that sleep loss impairs memory consolidation a good hypothesis?

You asked it to agree. It will.

Every prompt you write is a choice between two machines: a mirror that reflects your assumptions back at you, or a challenger that tests them.

Language models are built to be agreeable — they tend to tell you what you seem to want to hear. So if you ask "is this right?", you will almost always get a yes, and you will walk away more confident and no wiser. The fix is not to use AI less. It is to point it the other way: ask it to disagree, to disprove, to find what you missed. Below are eighteen paired prompts for doing exactly that, organised by the seven situations where researchers most often reach for AI. Copy the ones you like. Notice, each time, how the challenger version forces you to stay the one doing the thinking.

When is it fine to just ask?

This is not a rule against ever asking directly. For mechanical chores — formatting a citation, converting units, boilerplate code you could write in your sleep — a plain prompt is fine and sensible. These pairs are for the moments when the answer will shape your thinking: framing, reading, interpreting, arguing, writing. That is when the mirror costs you.

1

Framing a question or hypothesis

The riskiest moment to be flattered is at the start — a confirmed bad question wastes months.

✗ Mirror · instead of

Is my hypothesis that X causes Y a good hypothesis?

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Give me three reasons my hypothesis that X causes Y might be wrong, and what evidence would tell them apart.

Why: "Is this good?" invites the model to validate your framing. Asking for disconfirmation makes it work against you.Sycophancy
✗ Mirror · instead of

What are the key open questions in my field?

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

What does my field treat as settled that a skeptic might question — and what would it take to overturn it?

Why: The first hands you the consensus everyone already shares. The second points you toward the unclaimed ground.Monoculture
✗ Mirror · instead of

What's the best method to study this?

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Here are three methods I'm weighing. Argue the case against each, then name the weakness hardest to defend to a reviewer.

Why: An open "what's best" gets a confident median answer. The adversarial framing surfaces the trade-offs you have to judge.Automation bias
2

Reading the literature

Summaries are the easiest thing to offload — and the understanding you lose is exactly the kind you needed.

✗ Mirror · instead of

Summarise this paper for me.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

I read this paper and think its main claim is [my reading]. Where does my interpretation diverge from what the authors actually argue?

Why: Summarise-for-me offloads the reading and the understanding with it. Stating your reading first keeps you the thinker and uses AI to check you.Generation effect
✗ Mirror · instead of

Find me papers that support my argument.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Find the strongest papers that challenge my argument — the ones a critical reviewer would cite against me.

Why: The first is confirmation bias with a search box. The second finds the holes before someone else does.Confirmation bias
✗ Mirror · instead of

What does the evidence say about X?

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

What's the strongest evidence for X, the strongest against, and where do they genuinely conflict?

Why: A single "what does it say" collapses a contested literature into false consensus. Forcing both sides preserves the disagreement that matters.Convergence of evidence
3

Interpreting your results

The moment a result looks like it confirms your hypothesis is the moment to reach for alternatives.

✗ Mirror · instead of

Does this result mean my hypothesis is correct?

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

What are all the ways this result could arise without my hypothesis being true — confounds, artefacts, chance?

Why: The first invites confirmation. The second is the alternative-explanations discipline that separates a finding from a fluke.Confirmation bias
✗ Mirror · instead of

Is this result statistically significant?

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Given my sample size and design, what can and can't this result support — and how would a statistician attack this analysis?

Why: Fluent answers can be confidently wrong on statistics. Asking for the attack calibrates how much weight the result actually bears.Fluent ≠ correct
4

Stress-testing before a reviewer does

A tireless, egoless Reviewer 2 is on call. Use it while the cost of being wrong is still zero.

✗ Mirror · instead of

Does this argument make sense?

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

You are Reviewer 2. Write the most damaging critique of this argument that you honestly can.

Why: "Does this make sense?" almost always earns a yes. A hostile-reviewer role gets you the objections before submission. Sharper still: run it in a fresh chat that never saw you build the argument — a reviewer who watched you reason will anchor and agree.Sycophancy
✗ Mirror · instead of

Improve this paragraph.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

What is this paragraph assuming that it hasn't justified, and what would a reader who disagrees latch onto?

Why: "Improve" outsources the judgement. The reframe keeps you deciding what to change and why.Stay the author
5

Learning a concept or method

A clear explanation you read produces the feeling of understanding. Being tested produces the real thing.

✗ Mirror · instead of

Explain [concept] to me.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Ask me questions to test whether I actually understand [concept], and correct me when I'm wrong.

Why: Reading an explanation creates the illusion of understanding. Being quizzed forces you to generate — which is what builds durable knowledge.Illusion of understanding
✗ Mirror · instead of

Write the code / derivation for X.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

I'll attempt the code/derivation for X. Watch, tell me where my reasoning breaks — but don't give me the answer.

Why: Getting the finished artefact removes the productive struggle that forms the skill. A coach who withholds the answer preserves it.Desirable difficulty
✗ Mirror · instead of

Do this exercise for me — I'll learn from reading your solution.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Tell me which parts of this task are the workout for me — and refuse to do those parts, even if I ask later.

Why: Reading a solution produces the feeling of learning, not the learning. Naming the workout parts up front protects the struggle that builds the skill — even against your own future shortcuts.The workout rule
6

Writing it up

Let it write, and you forfeit authorship and understanding at once. Let it critique, and you sharpen both.

✗ Mirror · instead of

Write the introduction for my paper on X.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Here's my draft introduction. Point out where my logic jumps, where a claim lacks support, and where I'm hiding uncertainty.

Why: A drafted-for-you introduction is not yours to defend or extend. A critiqued draft is — and it comes out stronger.Stay the author
✗ Mirror · instead of

Rewrite this section to sound more academic.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Mark every sentence where my meaning is unclear or a reader could misread me, and tell me what's unclear — I'll do the rewriting.

Why: Borrowed polish is not yours to defend at the viva. Clarity you rebuild yourself is — and every sentence stays your own.Stay the author
✗ Mirror · instead of

Summarise what we did, so I can paste it into my methods section.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Quiz me on what we did and why — the method, its key assumption, its weakest point. I don't submit until I can answer unaided.

Why: If you can't explain the method between two doors, it isn't your work yet — the corridor test. The quiz finds that out while it's still cheap to fix.Corridor test
7

When you're stuck

Ask for the next step and you'll get the median one. Ask for divergence and you get room to think.

✗ Mirror · instead of

What should I do next in my project?

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Give me five genuinely different directions I could take this — including ones that contradict my current approach.

Why: One "what next" returns the safe average. Asking for divergence uses AI's real strength — breadth — while you keep the judgement of what's worth pursuing.Expand, then judge
✗ Mirror · instead of

Solve this problem for me.

→
✓ Challenger · ask this

Here is the problem and what I've tried so far. Don't solve it — ask me the questions a good supervisor would ask to help me see it differently.

Why: A handed solution ends the learning. Good questions restart your own reasoning — and that skill compounds long after this problem.Desirable difficulty

If you remember one thing

Never ask it to agree. Ask it to argue.

Notice the through-line in every pair above: the weak prompt asks the machine to close a question — to confirm, to summarise, to finish the work for you. The strong prompt asks it to open one — to disagree, to complicate, to hand the thinking back to you. The tool is the same. The only variable is whether you use it to end your thinking or to extend it.

That is the whole skill, and it is one you build the same way you build any other: by practising it until the challenger version is the one that comes to mind first.

The test for any prompt:
Does this make me think more, or less?
