The Practice Room

Reading the guide produced the feeling of understanding. This page is where you find out. Nine research scenarios, honest confidence ratings — then one experiment on your own hypothesis.

Format  9 scenarios · ~15 minutes Grading  none — this is practice, not assessment Privacy  nothing leaves your browser

The guide's own Part IV explains why this page exists: you remember what you retrieve and generate, not what you reread. So don't reread — answer.

1CommitEach scenario is a situation you will actually face. Pick the move you would really make — before anything is revealed.
2Rate your confidenceSay how sure you are. At the end you'll see how well your confidence tracked your accuracy — calibration is a skill of its own.
3Reveal & rereadGet the reasoning, not just the answer — and a pointer to the part of the guide worth revisiting if it surprised you.

One rule: commit before you peek. The learning is in the retrieval — reveal first, and this page becomes just more reading.

The final step

Now do it for real: the two-prompt experiment

Scenarios are rehearsal. This is the real thing — run the mirror and the challenger on your own hypothesis, in your own AI tool, and watch the difference with your own eyes. Takes about ten minutes.

1Write your hypothesis

One or two sentences. A working hypothesis, an interpretation of a result, a claim your thesis depends on — whatever you're currently most attached to. (Attachment is the point.)

2Copy both prompts

They update as you type above. Same hypothesis, opposite jobs.

✗ The mirror

✓ The challenger

3Run both — separately

Open your AI tool of choice and send each prompt in a fresh conversation, so the second answer isn't shaped by the first. Read the two answers side by side. Notice what the mirror's answer does to your confidence — and what it adds to your understanding. (Need more challenger phrasings? Steal from the phrasebook.)

4Write down what happened

Three sentences are enough. Your notes are saved in this browser, and only in this browser — use the copy button to move them into your own notes or lab book.

If nothing stung: be suspicious. Either your hypothesis is unusually strong — or your challenger prompt let it off easy. Sharpen the prompt ("attack the assumption I am most likely to be blind to") and run it again. A challenger that finds nothing is usually a mirror wearing a costume.

Before you go

Nine scenarios is a start, not a certificate.

These situations will read differently once they have happened to you — the seminar dismissal, the too-perfect paper, the chatbot's warm "excellent insight." Come back before your next supervision meeting or journal-club and run the room again. The habit is formed the day the challenger version is the one that comes to mind first.
